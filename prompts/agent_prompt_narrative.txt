It is Monday morning and the SouthPark Centre file has landed on my desk. I am a second-year
acquisitions associate at Harborview Capital Partners (Fund III), and this is mine to run to
Investment Committee by Friday. There is a broker package in the data room, a string of emails
from my VP and the partners, three lender term sheets, and a market-research folder. By the end of
the week I owe a full underwriting model, an IC recommendation, and, if it's a Go, a signed-out
LOI. There is also a second, smaller deal (Ballantyne) my VP wants me to glance at and clear off
the pipeline.

The way I'll work this deal: I build everything in the provided workbook at
/tmp/outputs/southpark_centre_underwriting.xlsx (a structured shell with the tabs and row labels
already laid out, I fill it in, I don't rename it), I keep the firm's running pipeline tracker
current as each deal moves stages, and I recalculate and check my own model before I trust a
number. I need to read the emails in order first, to understand the chronologically of info and there are some new requests and instructions changing around this deal.

The Offering Memorandum will quote a headline occupancy, a trailing NOI, and a cap rate, and my whole job in the first phase is
to figure out where those are soft. So I pull the rent roll and I open every lease, one tenant at a
time, and I check the rent roll against what the leases actually say. Somewhere in here there will
be a tenant the broker is still counting who has quietly gone dark, and a rent on the roll that the
lease does not support, I need to find those myself and decide how they move the real occupancy and
the real in-place income. I also read the other-income lines skeptically: a one-time fee is not
income I can underwrite, and an antenna contract that has half expired is not the number on the OM.
I record the broker-stated figures next to my reconciled figures so the IC can see exactly what I
changed and why. Then I log both deals on the tracker at their right stage.

When the Ballantyne package shows up, I need to work out the implied going-in cap, layer on the broker's growth to get an unlevered return, and then lever it
to an equity return using the cost of debt and LTV my VP quoted. I compare that to the fund's
minimum and write the honest two-line answer. 

With the deal real, I go to the market. I read the submarket report and reproduce its facts into my
model so my assumptions are anchored to something defensible, and I work through the comparable
sales and the comparable leases, there are a lot of them, and most are noise: crisis-era trades,
the wrong submarket, the wrong asset type, incomplete data. I have to judge each one and keep only
what's truly comparable, and I note why for the rest. Out of the lease comps I'm really trying to
answer one question: what does space like this actually rent for today, by suite size, so I can mark
each in-place tenant to market and see where the upside is.

Now I underwrite tenant by tenant. Our standard leasing guide gives me default renewal
probabilities, downtime, TI, and commissions by suite-size bucket; I apply those, and I deviate only
where a comp tells me to and I can defend it. The big vacancy, the floors the anchor is giving back, I plan to re-demise into smaller spec suites and lease up over time, with a buildout budget and a
schedule. A couple of near-term rollovers I treat probabilistically rather than pretending I know.
This is where I set the assumptions the whole model will run on.

Then I build the proforma, month by month over the hold. Contractual rent with escalations, the
expected-value blend where tenants might roll, NNN reimbursements, the other income, operating
expenses grown at inflation, the management fee (which is circular off EGI, so I handle that), down
to NOI. I subtract the capital plan, renovation, ongoing TI and commissions, reserves, to get to
unlevered cash flow, and I size the exit off a forward-looking NOI and an exit cap. I want to see an
unlevered return before any leverage so I know whether the real estate works on its own.

Midweek my VP sends revisions: a different purchase price to run as the base case, a different spec-
suite TI allowance, and a shift in the buildout timing. This is the real test of whether I built the
model right, if it's properly linked, I change three inputs and everything downstream moves
correctly, and I verify that it did rather than assuming.

Financing next. I have three term sheets, but one lender is effectively out, my capital-markets
contact has flagged they have no capacity, so however good their terms look, selecting them would be
a mistake. I underwrite the two real options side by side: size each loan against total project
cost, accrue the construction interest, find the month it converts to permanent, and compute the
levered return each one produces. I pick the lender that actually delivers the better levered return
and document why. Then I extend the cash flow with the debt service and the levered cash flow.

Finally the equity and the package. I run the JV waterfall, return of capital, the preferred
return, the GP catch-up, and the residual promote split, month by month, and I read off the LP and
GP returns. I will not trust it until my sense-checks tie: distributions equal what's available
every month, the partners' cash flows sum back to the deal's levered cash flow, and all capital and
preferred are fully returned by exit. I stress the deal across purchase price, exit cap, rent
growth, and cost and lease-up risk in sensitivity tables. Then I write the IC summary with every
number linked back to the model, corrected occupancy, reconciled NOI, the debt I chose and why, the
returns, and a clear Go or No-Go measured against the fund's criteria. If it's a Go, I draft the LOI
off the terms in my VP's email and move the deal to LOI Sent.

I derive the figures myself, the real occupancy, the reconciled income, how the lines forecast, what
the lenders size to, what the partners earn, and whether this clears our bar. The deterministic
grader checks the model I produce (the data deliverables, the reconciliations, the debt and
waterfall, and the sense-checks that must tie), so the model has to be recalculated and internally
consistent before I call it done. Pinned house assumptions (the fund's standard rates and the
guide's defaults) I take as given; everything else is mine to work out.
